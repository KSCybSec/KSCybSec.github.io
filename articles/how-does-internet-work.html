How Does the
Internet Actually Work?

You type a website address, press Enter, and a few seconds later a page appears. But what actually happened in between?

Let's try something simple.

Open your browser.

Type a website address.

https://example.com

Press Enter.

A moment later, the website appears.

It feels almost instant.

But your computer didn't magically know where that website was.

It had to figure out where to send your request, send the request across a network, find the right computer, and receive a response.

Think about it

If the Internet is made of millions of devices, how does your computer know where to go?

Let's follow one request

Imagine you want to visit a website.

You type the address and press Enter.

From your point of view, that's it.

Behind the scenes, a small journey begins.

One simple request
Your device
→
Network
→
Internet
→
Web server

Your request travels through several systems before reaching the server that can answer it.

And the interesting part?

The response has to make its way back too.

Your computer isn't alone

Your laptop or phone is connected to a network.

Maybe that's your home Wi-Fi.

Maybe it's a mobile network.

Maybe you're connected to a university or company network.

That network gives your device a way to communicate with other devices.

But your home network can't contain the entire Internet.

Somewhere beyond it are other networks, routers, servers and countless other devices.

The Internet isn't one giant computer. It's a network of networks.

So how does a device know where to send something?

This is where addresses become important.

Computers communicating across networks need addresses that help identify where data should go.

These are called IP addresses.

You don't need to memorise all the details yet.

For now, think of an IP address like an address that helps network traffic find the right destination.

A small clue

But when you type a website name, do you really type an IP address?

Usually, no.

You type something much easier to remember: a domain name.

That's where another important piece of the Internet appears.

The Internet has a name problem

Imagine having to remember the numerical address of every website you wanted to visit.

That would get annoying very quickly.

Instead, we use names.

You type something like:

example.com

But somewhere along the way, your device needs to discover the network address associated with that name.

This is where DNS comes in.

DNS — the Domain Name System — helps translate human-friendly domain names into information that computers can use to locate the destination.

Think of it like asking:

“I know the name. Where is this?”

Once your device has the information it needs, the request can continue its journey.

Your request has to travel

Now your request needs to get from your device to the destination.

It doesn't simply travel in one straight line.

Along the way, network devices called routers help move traffic between different networks.

A router looks at information about where traffic needs to go and forwards it toward the next destination.

Your request might pass through several network devices before reaching the server.

You don't see any of this happening.

You simply see the website load.

The Internet hides an enormous amount of complexity behind a very simple experience.

Eventually, your request reaches a server

Somewhere on the other side is a computer prepared to respond to requests.

That's the basic idea behind a web server.

Your browser asks for something.

The server receives the request.

It processes it.

Then it sends a response back.

That response might contain HTML, images, stylesheets, JavaScript and other resources needed to build the page you're looking at.

Your browser takes those pieces and turns them into the website you see.

And then the journey happens again

Remember that request we sent?

The server's response has to travel back.

It moves through networks and devices until the information reaches your computer.

Your browser receives it.

It puts everything together.

And suddenly:

The website appears.

All of that can happen in a fraction of a second.

Now think like someone learning cybersecurity

At first, this might sound like networking.

And it is.

But cybersecurity starts becoming much more interesting when you ask questions about what happens along the way.

What if someone intercepts the traffic?

What if you connect to the wrong server?

What if someone redirects you somewhere malicious?

What if the server itself has a vulnerability?

What if someone is watching the network?

Suddenly, understanding how the Internet works becomes part of understanding how attacks work.

And that's why networking is one of the foundations of cybersecurity.

Remember this

When you visit a website, your device doesn't simply “go to the Internet.” It communicates across networks, finds a destination, sends a request, receives a response, and turns that response into the page you see.
