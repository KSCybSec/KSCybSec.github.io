How Cyber Attacks Actually Happen

Cyber attacks rarely begin with the dramatic moment people imagine. Usually, they begin with something much smaller — a message, a mistake, an exposed service, a stolen password, or a weakness someone discovers.

So how does an attack actually unfold?

Imagine a company notices something unusual on one of its computers. A new account appeared. A strange process is running. And the machine has started communicating with an unfamiliar server.

The important question isn't simply: “Was the system attacked?”

The better question is: “What happened, step by step?”

01 · Initial access

An employee receives an email that appears to come from a familiar service.

The message contains a link asking them to sign in. They click it and enter their credentials.

The attacker now has a possible way into the environment.

This is called initial access — the point where an attacker first gains a foothold or access to a target environment.

Phishing is one possible route. But it isn't the only one. Other routes can involve stolen credentials, exposed services, vulnerable software, malicious files, or compromised accounts.

02 · Execution

Getting access is not necessarily the same thing as accomplishing anything useful.

The attacker may need something to actually run: a command, script, malicious program, or another action.

This is where execution becomes important.

From a defender's perspective, this can create valuable clues: processes starting unexpectedly, unusual commands, scripts running at strange times, or applications behaving differently.

INVESTIGATOR'S QUESTION

A new process suddenly appears on a workstation. What would you want to know?

  • What process started?
  • Which user started it?
  • What launched it?
  • When did it start?
  • What did it communicate with?

03 · Persistence

An attacker may not want to lose access simply because the computer restarts or the current session ends.

They may attempt to maintain access through mechanisms that cause something to run again or preserve access to an account or system.

This is broadly known as persistence.

Important distinction

Persistence does not mean every unusual startup item or scheduled task is malicious. Legitimate software also creates services, scheduled tasks, and startup entries.

Context matters.

04 · Discovery

Once inside, an attacker may need to understand the environment.

What systems exist? Which accounts are available? What information is stored here? What other machines can be reached?

This stage is about learning about the environment.

05 · Impact

Eventually, an attack may reach its intended objective.

That objective depends on the attacker and the environment. It could involve stealing information, disrupting services, encrypting files, changing data, or abusing an account.

This is where the earlier concepts from the Beginner Path start connecting together.

But attacks aren't always this simple.

Real incidents can involve multiple systems, accounts, techniques, and stages.

An attacker might enter through phishing, move to another system, discover additional accounts, obtain higher privileges, access sensitive information, and attempt to hide their activity.

The exact sequence can vary. That's why defenders don't simply memorize one attack pattern.

They investigate the evidence left behind by activity.

THE DEFENDER'S VIEW

Imagine you are investigating the incident from the other side.

  • A suspicious email was received.
  • An account logged in unexpectedly.
  • A new process appeared.
  • The machine contacted an unfamiliar IP address.
  • Files were accessed shortly afterwards.

Each event by itself may not prove an attack. Together, however, they form a timeline worth investigating.

This is where cybersecurity becomes investigation.

The more pieces you understand, the easier it becomes to see the bigger picture.

The important lesson

A cyber attack is not usually one magical action.

It is a sequence of actions and events.

Attackers try to move from one step to another. Defenders try to detect, understand, interrupt, and contain those steps.

And every step can leave clues.

TAKEAWAY · BEGINNER PATH COMPLETE

Cyber attacks can involve initial access, execution, persistence, discovery, and impact. The exact path varies, but understanding the sequence helps defenders turn individual events into a meaningful investigation.
