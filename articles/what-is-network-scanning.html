What Is Network Scanning?

Before you can understand a machine, you need to know what it is exposing. Network scanning is one way security professionals begin that discovery.

NETWORKING RECONNAISSANCE ~5 MIN READ

You have an IP address.

You know the machine exists somewhere on the network.

But looking at an IP address alone tells you almost nothing about what is happening behind it.

Maybe port 80 is open. Maybe port 22 is listening. Maybe nothing is reachable at all.

The question is no longer “Where is the machine?”

It's: “What can I discover about it?”

What is network scanning?

Network scanning is the process of sending network requests or probes to discover information about hosts and their network exposure.

Depending on the type of scan, you might be trying to discover:

HOSTS
Which devices appear to be reachable on a network?
PORTS
Which communication endpoints appear to be open or closed?
SERVICES
What network services appear to be running?
VERSIONS
What software or service versions may be present?
NETWORK STRUCTURE
What can be inferred about the systems and paths around a target?

Scanning is about questions

A useful way to think about scanning is not as “running a tool.”

Think of it as asking a series of questions.

Is the host there?
→
What ports respond?
→
What services are there?

Each answer gives you another piece of the picture.

This is why scanning is often associated with reconnaissance and enumeration.

Not every response means “open”

When a scanner probes a port, the result is not simply “the port exists.”

A port can appear open, closed, or filtered depending on how the host and network respond to the probe.

OPEN
A service appears to be listening and accepting the type of connection being tested.
CLOSED
The host is reachable, but no service appears to be listening on that port.
FILTERED
A firewall or other network control may be preventing the scanner from determining the port's state clearly.

These states are clues about how the network is behaving.

Why security teams scan networks

Network scanning isn't automatically an attack.

Administrators can scan their own environments to check whether systems are reachable and whether expected services are exposed.

Security teams can use scanning as part of authorized assessments to understand an organization's attack surface.

The same activity can look very different depending on context. Scanning a system you own or have explicit permission to test is fundamentally different from probing someone else's network without authorization.

In real security work, authorization and scope matter before technical capability.

What about attackers?

Attackers can also use scanning during reconnaissance.

They may be trying to discover which systems exist, which services are exposed, and where further investigation might be possible.

This is one reason defenders care about network visibility.

Unexpected scanning activity can become a useful signal when it is correlated with other network and security events.

PAUSE FOR A SECOND

If a machine has an open port, what would you want to know next: the port number, the service behind it, or both?

Scanning gives you clues, not answers

Suppose a scan tells you:

80 · OPEN
443 · OPEN
22 · OPEN

That's useful.

But the scan alone doesn't tell the complete story.

You still need to identify what services are actually running, what software they use, how they are configured, and whether the exposure is expected.

That's where enumeration becomes important.

The tool you'll meet next

There are many network-scanning tools, but one name appears repeatedly in cybersecurity: Nmap.

Nmap can discover hosts, scan ports, identify services, and perform other forms of network discovery depending on how it is configured.

But before using a tool, remember what the tool is actually doing.

→

Interpretation

The tool produces observations. The security professional turns those observations into understanding.

And that's exactly what we'll do next.

TAKEAWAY · 026

Network scanning is a way to discover what hosts and services are exposed on a network. It can reveal reachable hosts, port states, and possible services. Scanning is an important reconnaissance technique, but the results are clues that require interpretation. Always perform scanning only on systems and networks you are authorized to test.
