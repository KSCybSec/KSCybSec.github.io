LAN, WAN & the Internet

A network can be a few devices in one room. It can also connect systems across a city, a country, or the entire world. So where does one network end and another begin?

NETWORKING · ~5 MIN READ · CLUE 017

You're sitting at home.

Your laptop is connected to Wi-Fi. Your phone is connected too. Maybe your TV and printer are online as well.

They can communicate without leaving your house.

Now you open a website hosted on a server somewhere on the other side of the world.

Suddenly, your little home network is talking to something much, much bigger.

So how does that happen?

The important thing to understand is that LAN, WAN and the Internet are not three completely separate worlds.

They're different ways of describing networks and how widely those networks are connected.

Start with the network around you

Think about your home Wi-Fi.

Your phone, laptop, smart TV and other devices may all connect through the same router.

They form a LAN — Local Area Network.

"Local" is the important word.

A LAN covers a relatively small area such as a home, classroom, office, laboratory or building.

Inside this LAN, the devices can communicate with each other without every conversation needing to travel across the entire Internet.

That's useful.

Your laptop doesn't need to send a print job halfway around the world just because the printer is connected to a network.

Now make the distance bigger

Imagine a company with offices in Chennai, Mumbai and Delhi.

Each office might have its own local network.

But the company may also need those offices to communicate with each other.

That's where a WAN — Wide Area Network becomes useful.

A WAN connects networks across a larger geographical area.

The distance could span different buildings, cities, regions or even countries.

Notice something interesting?

The WAN doesn't replace the local networks. It connects them.

And then there's the Internet

Here's where the name can become confusing.

The Internet isn't one giant computer sitting somewhere in a secret building.

It's a massive collection of interconnected networks.

In fact, that's where the name comes from: inter-network — networks connected to other networks.

Your home network connects to your Internet Service Provider. That network connects to other networks, which connect to still more networks.

Eventually, your request can reach a server somewhere else in the world.

There isn't necessarily one single path that every packet must take. Networking equipment helps move traffic toward its destination.

STOP & THINK

Your laptop is in a small home LAN. The website is on another network thousands of kilometres away. What connects the two?

Routers are part of the answer

You've already seen a router in the previous article.

A router helps move traffic between networks.

Your home router can connect your local network to your ISP's network.

Other routers help move traffic further through the wider network.

So when your laptop sends data to a remote server, the journey can involve several networks and several pieces of networking equipment.

LAN
A relatively small local network such as a home, office or lab.
WAN
A network that connects systems or smaller networks across larger distances.
INTERNET
A global system of interconnected networks.

Why does this matter in cybersecurity?

Because an attacker doesn't interact with "the Internet" as one giant thing.

They interact with actual networks, devices, addresses, routes and services.

A defender needs to understand the same landscape.

If an unusual connection appears in a company's logs, someone might need to ask:

Where did it come from? Which network was involved? Which device sent it? Where was it going?

The more clearly you understand the network, the easier those questions become.

Go back to that simple moment from the beginning.

You're sitting at home. Your laptop is on your Wi-Fi. You open a website.

What looked like one click is actually a conversation crossing several networks.

Your small network has just reached the rest of the world.

TAKEAWAY
LAN describes a local network, WAN connects networks across larger distances, and the Internet is a massive collection of interconnected networks.
