The device that connects networks

A router is a device that connects different networks and forwards packets between them.

Your home router is a simple example. It connects your local network to your Internet service provider's network.

The router does not magically know the entire path to every device. Instead, it examines where a packet needs to go and uses its routing information to decide where to send it next.

The default gateway is usually a router interface on the local network.

If your destination is outside your local subnet, your device can send the packet toward that gateway.

Routers use routing tables

A router needs information that helps it decide where packets should go.

This information is stored in a routing table.

The important idea is: a router compares a packet's destination with its routing information and chooses an appropriate next hop or interface.

What is a next hop?

A router does not necessarily send a packet directly to its final destination.

Instead, it may send the packet to another router.

Each router makes another forwarding decision. Eventually, the packet reaches a network that contains the destination.

Why does this matter in security?

Routers sit at important boundaries between networks.

Because they control how traffic moves between those networks, their configuration can have major security consequences.

Routing determines where traffic can travel. Security controls such as firewalls and access-control rules can then determine which traffic is allowed to cross certain boundaries.

During an investigation, network paths can also provide clues. A packet's source, destination, routing information, and surrounding network activity can help analysts understand how communication occurred.

DHCP helps a device join a network. Subnetting helps define where that network begins and ends. ARP helps with local IPv4 delivery. And routers connect different networks together.

But there is another problem waiting around the corner.

Your laptop may have a private address such as 192.168.1.24. The public Internet cannot simply treat that address like a globally unique address.

So how does a private network communicate with the public Internet?

TAKEAWAY · 022

Routers connect different networks and forward packets toward their destinations. Devices use a default gateway when traffic needs to leave the local subnet, while routers use routing information to choose the next hop.
