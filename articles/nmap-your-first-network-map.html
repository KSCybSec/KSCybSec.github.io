Nmap: Your First
Network Map

A network can contain many devices, services, and open doors. Nmap helps you turn that invisible network into something you can inspect.

NETWORKING ~5 MIN READ LEARN · TRY · THINK

So far, we've been following the clues one at a time.

A device has an IP address. A service listens on a port. A network connects many devices together.

But there's a problem.

What if you don't know what's actually there?

Maybe you're responsible for a small lab network. Maybe you're checking whether a server is exposing an unexpected service. Or maybe you're simply trying to understand your own network.

Instead of checking everything manually, you can use a tool designed to ask the network questions.

That tool is Nmap.

SCAN
TARGET
IP 192.168.1.20
PORT 443
SERVICE HTTPS
PROBE → RESPONSE → INTERPRET

Think of Nmap as a way of turning a network into a map.

You give it a target that you're authorized to examine. Nmap sends probes and observes the responses. Those responses become clues about the target.

So, what is Nmap?

Nmap stands for Network Mapper.

It is a network discovery and security auditing tool. It can help identify hosts, discover ports, and determine what network services may be available.

That makes it especially useful for understanding the attack surface of a network.

Notice the wording: understanding the attack surface.

Nmap doesn't automatically mean "hacking." Security teams, administrators, students, and penetration testers all use network scanning to understand systems they are authorized to examine.

Your first scan

Imagine you have a device in your own lab at:

EXAMPLE TARGET
192.168.1.20

A basic Nmap scan could look like this:

AUTHORIZED LAB EXAMPLE
nmap 192.168.1.20

Only scan systems you own or have explicit permission to test.

Nmap then starts asking questions about the target.

Is the host reachable? Which ports respond? What services appear to be listening?

The exact output depends on the target and the scan, but a simplified result might look like this:

PORT STATE SERVICE
22/tcp open ssh
80/tcp open http
443/tcp open https

Suddenly, the machine isn't just an IP address anymore.

You have clues.

What did Nmap actually find?

Let's slow down.

22/tcp

Port 22 is commonly associated with SSH, a protocol used for secure remote administration.

80/tcp

Port 80 is commonly associated with HTTP, the protocol used for ordinary web traffic.

443/tcp

Port 443 is commonly associated with HTTPS, the encrypted version of HTTP used by most modern websites.

These are conventions, not guarantees. A service can be configured to listen on a different port.

That's why a scan is only the beginning of an investigation.

The map becomes a trail

Imagine you're responsible for a small company server.

You know its IP address. You know it is supposed to run a website.

You scan it.

The result shows:

80/tcp open http
443/tcp open https

That makes sense. The server is supposed to host a website.

But suppose you also find another service that nobody expected.

Now the scan has created a question.

Why is this service exposed?

That is where cybersecurity thinking begins.

The scan didn't tell you that something is vulnerable. It gave you a clue worth investigating.

"Host is up" is useful too

Nmap can also tell you whether a target appears reachable.

You may see a message indicating that the host is up.

This matters because an IP address existing on paper doesn't necessarily mean a device is currently responding.

So your investigation starts becoming a simple chain:

HOST UP The device responds
PORT OPEN Something is accepting connections
SERVICE KNOWN A likely service is identified

Each answer gives you another piece of the map.

Why defenders care about Nmap

Scanning isn't only useful for people trying to break into systems.

A defender can use it to check whether a machine is exposing services that shouldn't be there.

For example:

A server is supposed to provide HTTPS. A scan shows HTTPS. Everything looks expected.

But another administrative service is also reachable from a network where it wasn't supposed to be exposed.

The scan has just helped reveal an unexpected attack surface.

This is one reason visibility matters in defensive security. You can't investigate what you don't know exists.

Don't stop at the port

Here's an important habit.

Don't look at an Nmap result and immediately think:

"Open port = vulnerability."

That's not how it works.

An open port usually means that something is listening and accepting connections.

Whether that service is configured safely, outdated, exposed unnecessarily, or vulnerable requires further investigation.

Nmap gives you the map. It doesn't finish the investigation for you.

TAKEAWAY

Nmap helps turn an unknown network into something you can inspect. It can reveal reachable hosts, open ports, and likely services.

The important skill isn't memorizing commands. It's learning to look at the results and ask: What does this clue tell me?
